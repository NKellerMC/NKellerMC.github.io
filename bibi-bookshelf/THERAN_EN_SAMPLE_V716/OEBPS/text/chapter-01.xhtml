CHAPTER 1
THE SILENCE OF THE SEA

Outside, it was still snowing, fine flakes drifting past the kitchen window and settling on rooftops that were already white. That morning, James heard the television before he noticed the smell of coffee, and he dropped his backpack beside the kitchen door with a dull thud.

“You're going to break that zipper one of these days,” said his mother, Emma Chapman, flipping the eggs in the pan.

“It's fine.”

“For now.”

James pulled out his usual chair, the one that let him see the television without turning his head. The weather forecast was ending.

“...clear skies along nearly the entire coast. The observed variations remain within Nich parameters...”

Sarah came running down the stairs while she was still tying back her hair. Her shirt had ridden up on one side, exposing a strip of translucent skin over her ribs. Without looking, she tugged the fabric back into place, walked past the table, took a piece of toast from James's plate, and kept going.

“Hey.”

“What?”

She had already taken her first bite.

“That was mine.”

“Now it's ours.”

Their mother pointed the spatula at the empty chair. Sarah made a face but came back.

“You take this whole breakfast thing way too seriously.”

“Someone in this house has to.”

James gave a crooked smile. The news music returned for a few seconds, then vanished, replaced by an aerial shot of the ocean. A helicopter flew above the water, and in the center of the screen a cargo ship sat motionless. The anchor drew a breath before speaking.

“We interrupt our regular programming with a breaking news update.”

James set his mug on the table as the camera moved closer to the ship. There was no smoke, no fire, no tugboats nearby. Just water in every direction.

“Maritime Guard teams have confirmed that a transport vessel lost the ability to move during the night, although its propulsion systems continue to respond to commands. So far, investigators have found no mechanical failure, collision, or maritime conditions capable of explaining the incident.”

Several crew members had gathered on deck. One of them was staring at the horizon.

“Did the engine break?” James asked.

her mother set the plates on the table.

“They'll figure it out soon.”

James kept watching the television.

“What if they don't?”

She shrugged.

“Then it stays under review until they do.”

Sarah took another piece of toast.

“It's a ship.”

James looked at her.

“I know.”

“Ships break.”

“But they said they couldn't find anything wrong with it.”

“Then they haven't found it yet.”

“Don't you think it's weird?”

Sarah finished chewing before she answered.

“I do. I just don't think it's impossible.”

“A ship that can't move even though the propulsion system works?”

“James, I don't even know how a ship's engine works. If the guy on TV doesn't know what happened, I definitely don't.”

“Fair.”

“Thank you.”

James looked back at the screen. The anchor had returned, this time with a map beside him.

“Authorities are also investigating similar reports recorded over the past few weeks.”

Their mother looked up.

“Weeks?”

James turned to her and realized that, this time, she was paying attention too. The anchor touched his earpiece and fell silent for a moment.

“We've just received another update.”

The map disappeared. When it returned, more points marked the ocean: one, then another, then a third, none of them close together. Sarah stopped chewing.

“Wait.”

“Authorities are asking the public to remain calm. The incidents have been provisionally classified as under review, and at present there is no evidence linking the cases.”

Their mother picked up the remote and turned up the volume. No one spoke. James reached for his mug and discovered that the coffee was already cold. He couldn't remember the last time he'd taken a sip.

“Can I skip school today?”

“No.”

“But...”

“James.”

That was enough. He understood. The three points remained on the map as their mother tapped the table twice.

“Come on. Both of you.”

Sarah got up first and finished her toast before grabbing her backpack.

“If this lasts until Friday, do you think they'll cancel the chemistry test?”

“Dream big,” James said.

“I have hope.”

James passed the television on his way out. The anchor was repeating:

“The incidents remain under review.”

When James opened the door, a gust of cold air came in with him. A few flakes were still falling, small enough to melt the moment they touched his coat. Their mother locked the house and, before they headed to the car, touched two fingers to Sarah's forehead and then James's.

“May Nich keep you.”

Sarah accepted the gesture without breaking stride, and James did the same. Emma only did that when something worried her, though she would never admit it. Sarah was already walking toward the car, stepping through the snow piled along the sidewalk, while James cast one last glance through the kitchen window. On the television, the helicopter was still circling the cargo ship. He got into the car and, as always, Sarah took the front seat.

“One day I'm sitting there.”

“When I'm absent.”

“So never.”

Sarah smiled as their mother started the car. The street ahead looked like any other morning: a bus at the stop, a bicycle courier, someone leaving the bakery with a bag of bread. Car tires cut dark tracks through the snow on the road.

“Still thinking about the ships?” Sarah asked.

“There are four now.”

“I know.”

“And you're calm.”

Sarah turned slightly in her seat.

“I'm not calm. I just don't know what you expect me to do about four ships that won't move.”

James didn't answer.

“You do this,” she continued. “You stare at something you don't understand like it's going to confess.”

“Sometimes it works.”

“When?”

James thought for a moment.

“I don't remember.”

“Excellent scientific method.”

Their mother laughed quietly.

“Something being strange is one thing. Knowing all four stopped for the same reason is another.”

“Did you two rehearse this?”

“We're the majority,” Sarah said.

The car stopped at a light. A few seconds later, the dashboard lit up on its own with the words BREAKING NEWS. Their mother raised the volume. It was the same anchor, but now he was speaking faster.

“Authorities have just confirmed a fourth case similar to those reported this morning. Teams have been dispatched to the site. This incident has also been classified as under review.”

Another point appeared over the ocean, and Sarah looked up from her phone.

“Okay.”

She put the device away.

“Now it's starting to get weird.”

James looked at her.

“Now?”

“I could still call three a coincidence.”

“And four changes everything?”

“No. But it makes the coincidence a lot worse.”

“That doesn't even make sense.”

“It did in my head.”

When the light changed, their mother drove on and turned off the display a few seconds later. The car fell silent as James watched people walking along the sidewalks, some with snow still clinging to the shoulders of their coats. A few were laughing, others were waiting for the bus, and a woman crossed the street carrying two bags.

“Mom...”

“Hmm?”

“How long can something stay under review?”

her mother thought before answering.

“As long as it needs to.”

“What if they never find an explanation they can close?”

“Then it might eventually be classified as unintegrated.”

James looked up.

“So unintegrated means no answer?”

her mother glanced at him in the rearview mirror.

“No. It means we don't have one that works yet. Maybe an answer exists that no one can reach. Maybe information is missing. The classification doesn't choose between the two.”

“So something could exist that no one will ever be able to explain.”

“It could.”

Sarah turned around.

“Congratulations. You just discovered the unknown.”

“I'm serious.”

“So am I. If we already knew everything, research wouldn't exist.”

Mom nodded.

“Exactly.”

James was quiet for a few seconds.

“Then why does everyone say ‘it's under review’ like that solves anything?”

her mother thought again.

“Because not having an answer yet is information too.”

James looked at her. The sentence made sense, which bothered him a little. The school parking lot appeared ahead, with parents dropping their children at the entrance and students crossing through the gate as his mother pulled over.

“Have a good day.”

Sarah opened the door.

“Bye, Mom.”

James did the same.

“Bye.”

They headed toward the entrance. As they passed reception, James saw a television mounted on the wall. The cargo ship was still there, in the same image and the same ocean. The hall was already full of students in a hurry, someone laughing loudly in the distance, someone else complaining about the math test, and several students gathered near the front office in front of another television.

“...teams continue to investigate the vessels that have lost the ability to navigate...”

A teacher walked past them.

“Come on, everyone. Class is about to start.”

One student kept looking at the screen for another second before walking away. The others did the same.

“James!”

Noah came running down the hall with his backpack hanging from one shoulder.

“Did you see the news?”

“Yeah.”

“My dad already solved it.”

James turned to him.

“Solved it?”

“Satellites.”

“What?”

“According to him, some problem with the satellites messed with navigation or communications. I didn't understand half of it.”

“Would that stop the engine?”

Noah opened his mouth, then stopped before answering.

“Good question.”

“Then your dad didn't solve it.”

“I'll let him know he lost the prize.”

James laughed.

“Does he work with that stuff?”

“No.”

“Then why did you believe him?”

Noah shrugged.

“Because he said it like he knew.”

James stared at him for a second.

“That's a terrible argument.”

“I know. Works pretty often, though.”

They entered the classroom while the teacher was still writing the date on the board. James sat near the window, opened his notebook, and copied the date when class began. He tried to pay attention, but a helicopter crossed the sky outside and his head came up at once. It was too small to identify and kept flying until it disappeared behind the buildings.

“James.”

James looked forward again and found the teacher staring at him.

“Sorry.”

A few people laughed. He lowered his eyes to his notebook as the teacher continued. A few minutes later, one phone buzzed, then another, then another. In less than five seconds, notifications sounded around the room, and the teacher stopped.

“Put your phones away, please.”

No one moved. Another phone buzzed. The teacher sighed before taking her own from her pocket. When she read the screen, her expression barely changed, but it changed enough for James to notice. She walked to the television in the corner and turned it on. The news appeared immediately, the map taking up almost the entire screen, now with many more points scattered across it.

“Authorities have confirmed new reports over the past several hours. The incidents will no longer be treated as isolated occurrences. International teams are already monitoring the situation.”

No one was talking, not even the students who normally paid no attention. A girl in the front asked:

“How many?”

“There is no consolidated number yet,” the anchor replied.

Phones began appearing on desks.

“This says seven.”

“I saw eight.”

“My mom sent me six.”

“My brother says there are more outside our coast.”

“How does he know?”

“I don't know. He sent it.”

“Then he doesn't know.”

Voices began crossing over one another, and Noah slowly turned toward James.

“My dad says it's satellites,” someone said.

“See?”

“Your dad started an epidemic.”

“Show some respect for the expert.”

The teacher picked up the remote and turned off the television.

“Enough.”

“But...”

“When there is confirmed information, you'll know.”

“But if there are already seven...”

“If there are seven confirmed cases, then there are seven confirmed cases. That doesn't mean you have enough information to explain why they happened.”

She set the remote on her desk.

“For now, it's under review.”

A boy near the window raised his hand.

“When does it stop being under review?”

“When there's enough information.”

“How much is enough?”

The teacher stared at him for a moment.

“Page forty-two.”

A few people laughed.

“Seriously. Where were we?”

Someone called out the page number, and books opened again. James opened his too. It took several minutes for the whispers to fade, until pens returned to paper, a chair scraped at the back of the room, and someone asked to borrow an eraser. James looked from the dark television to his notebook and back. He wrote two words and stopped. Outside the window, the sky remained clear, the trees in the courtyard swayed in the wind, and a group of students crossed the garden toward another building. The teacher asked a question, Noah raised his hand, and James looked back down at his notebook. In the corner of the room, the television remained black.
