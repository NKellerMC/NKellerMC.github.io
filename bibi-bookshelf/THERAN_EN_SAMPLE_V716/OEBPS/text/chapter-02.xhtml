CHAPTER 2
WHAT STILL WORKS

The bell announced the end of the last class, and within seconds the hallway filled again with backpacks and overlapping conversations, someone celebrating a canceled practice and someone else complaining about homework. James put his notebook away slowly while he waited for Sarah to leave her classroom. In the main hall, the television was still on, the map taking up nearly the entire screen and more points scattered across the ocean than there had been that morning. The red banner at the bottom read: CONFIRMED CASES: 7. Two students walked past without slowing down.

“Bet it was a cyberattack.”

“On a ship?”

“I don't know.”

Farther down the hall, another group was arguing about the same thing.

“My uncle works at the port. He said the whole system froze.”

“My dad said it must be something to do with Nich.”

“Then why only some ships?”

The boy shrugged.

“They're investigating it.”

That ended the conversation. James watched the two of them until they disappeared into the crowd. A few hours earlier, three ships with no explanation had brought an entire classroom to a stop in front of a television. Now there were seven, and behind him someone was already complaining about the math test.

“You walk too fast.”

Sarah appeared beside him, adjusting the backpack on her shoulder.

“You took too long.”

“The teacher decided to explain half the lesson again.”

She followed James's gaze to the television.

“Still on this?”

“Seven now.”

Sarah looked back at the screen.

“I saw.”

“And no one seems to care.”

She subtly pointed to a boy debating the news while tying his shoe.

“They care. They just don't know what to do. Were you expecting an alarm?”

“No.”

“Then.”

“It's not ‘then.’ It went from three to seven in a few hours.”

“I know. It's strange. I'm just not going to stare at the TV until it confesses.”

James turned to her.

“You stole my line.”

“I improved it.”

Sarah started walking, and James followed.

Outside, the snow had stopped, but it still covered the cars and the edges of the sidewalks. Otherwise, the street looked the same as it had that morning. Buses went by, stores stayed open, and people came out of the market carrying bags. A few windows had the news playing, though hardly anyone stopped to watch. At the corner newsstand, James read the headline as they passed: SHIPS STOP WITHOUT EXPLANATION.

“Does this count as it still going on?”

“It counts as a headline,” Sarah said. “Don't get carried away.”

No one was home when they arrived. James dropped his backpack beside the couch. From the kitchen came only the steady hum of the refrigerator and the periodic click of the DT unit above the window. A note held to the door by a star-shaped magnet read: There's food in the pot. Back at six.

“Mom”

James smiled faintly because she had sent him the exact same message on his phone. He went upstairs to his room, opened the window, sat at his desk, and tried to start his math homework. He solved the first problem, read the second twice, and by the third realized he was no longer paying attention. He turned on the television. One channel blamed an electronic failure, the next solar activity, and another magnetic interference, but all of them ended with the same conclusion:

“...any conclusion would be premature. The incidents remain under review.”

James turned it off. In the silence, the television's explanations sounded even less convincing. Still, he went back to math and stayed there until he heard the front door open sometime later.

“James?”

“Up here.”

“Mom's not back yet?”

“No.”

“Is there food?”

“In the pot.”

“Great.”

Soon after, he heard the microwave, then the television. He tried to ignore both until Sarah called from the living room:

“Eleven.”

James stood.

“What?”

When he reached the living room, Sarah pointed at the television. CONFIRMED CASES: 11 ran along the bottom of the screen while a commentator spoke calmly above the banner.

“At present, there is no reason for the public to alter its routine. The incidents remain under review.”

Sarah picked up the remote and changed the channel. A cartoon appeared.

“Enough.”

James remained standing.

“Aren't you curious?”

“I am. But I've already heard four people say four different things with the exact same look of certainty.”

“There were seven. Now there are eleven.”

“I know. And that still doesn't tell me why.”

Sarah left the cartoon on and sank into the couch.

“Let me know when someone actually knows something.”

“Who?”

“Anyone.”

James stood in front of the colorful screen for another moment before returning to his room. This time he finished two more problems. A key turned in the door shortly before six.

“I'm home!”

Their mother came in and set her bag on a chair.

“If anyone asks, I survived another day.”

Sarah raised one hand from the couch.

“Good metric.”

“Thank you.”

James appeared in the kitchen.

“Did people talk about the ships at work?”

her mother opened the pot.

“A lot.”

“And?”

“And no one knows anything.”

“There are eleven now.”

“I saw.”

Sarah appeared behind him, searched a cabinet, and found a package of cookies.

“At school everyone was just guessing too. Hacker, system, satellite...”

“I bet they invent aliens tomorrow.”

Their mother laughed.

“Tomorrow? You're giving the internet too much credit.”

Sarah pointed the package at her.

“True.”

James didn't laugh.

“What if no one figures it out?”

her mother replaced the lid on the pot.

“Then tomorrow it still won't have an answer.”

“That's it?”

“For today, that's it.”

Sarah took a cookie.

“Wow. The household philosophy is getting worse.”

“Not helping,” James said.

“Wasn't trying to.”

Their mother began setting plates on the table.

“Let's eat.”

For several minutes, they talked about other things: the math test, one of Sarah's teachers, and a bill their mother had almost forgotten to pay. The television was still on in the living room, too quiet to make out clearly, until one sentence reached the kitchen.

“...seventeen confirmed vessels...”

James's fork stopped. Sarah had heard it too.

“Seventeen?”

Their mother looked toward the living room. The map was back on the screen, now with more points and none of them close together. CONFIRMED CASES: 17. Sarah chewed slowly.

“This is getting big, isn't it?”

“It is,” their mother said.

There was no drama in her voice and no change in her tone. She was simply acknowledging what she saw. James kept staring at the television.

“There were eleven a few hours ago.”

This time, Sarah didn't look away either.

“It's going up fast.”

Their mother picked up the remote.

“And we still don't know whether the number of cases is rising or just the number of confirmations.”

“There's still no explanation,” James said.

“No.”

She turned off the television.

“Why?” Sarah asked.

“Because we already know everything that report knows.”

Two clicks, and the screen went black. No one spoke for a few seconds, until Sarah pointed at James's plate.

“You going to eat that?”

“Yes.”

“Then eat it.”

“You're just waiting for me to give up.”

“That too.”

James pulled his plate closer, and the conversation changed without ceremony. After dinner, Sarah returned to the couch with her phone, their mother started washing the dishes, and James stood in the living room for a few seconds, facing the dark television.

“I'm going to do my homework.”

Sarah didn't look up.

“It's a miracle.”

“Very funny.”

“You already used that today.”

“Still works.”

James went upstairs, sat at his desk, and opened his math notebook. Outside the window, cars passed, someone walked a dog, and the lights in the houses came on one by one. Everything was still working. He returned to the problem when a phone buzzed somewhere in the house, then another, then another. James raised his head when he heard his mother's voice downstairs.

“Probably more updates.”

He waited, but no footsteps came. Sarah didn't move from the couch, her mother kept washing the dishes, and James didn't go downstairs either. He picked up his pencil, wrote the first number in the equation, stopped, erased it, and tried again. Downstairs, a phone buzzed once more. No one went to look.
